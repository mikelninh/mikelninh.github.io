How the Commons works

Preparedness should be understandable, testable and shared.

The simulator is intentionally simple enough for non-specialists, but its structure mirrors a serious resilience program: map assets, assume compromise, measure blast radius, introduce deterministic boundaries, test recovery and keep evidence.

01 · MAP

Know what matters.

Identify critical identities, services, data, external dependencies and recovery paths. Unknown ownership and unknown dependencies are risks in themselves.

02 · ASSUME

Start after something went wrong.

Some scenarios intentionally begin with a stolen session or compromised endpoint. Resilience is about limiting what happens next, not pretending prevention is perfect.

03 · CONTAIN

Measure the blast radius.

Track which identities, services and datasets the synthetic path reaches before an explicit boundary stops it.

04 · RECOVER

Make restoration a practiced capability.

Backups, session revocation, isolation and communications matter only when teams can use them under pressure.

05 · PROVE

Evidence beats checkboxes.

In a real program, “MFA enabled” is not enough. Run an authorized test that demonstrates the intended effect is actually blocked or contained.

06 · SHARE

Make good patterns public.

The Commons should help municipalities and small organizations reuse strong defensive patterns instead of solving the same problems alone.

Why Berlin is the first synthetic city case.

The public discussion around Berlin’s 2026 ransomware incident is a reminder that cyber resilience is civic infrastructure. This simulator does not model Berlin’s actual network, systems or vulnerabilities. Berlin is simply the first public-interest story we use to make blast radius, containment and recovery understandable to everyone.
