Your Brain Has 27 Tabs Open

A tiny experiment in noticing what's happening when your mind feels very, very busy.

By The Journal · 15 October 2026 · 4 min read
Back to the issue

Imagine your brain is a browser.

You have a maths assignment open. A message from a friend. A song you can't stop playing. A question about the universe. A memory of something awkward you said at lunch.

Twenty-seven tabs, at least.

And then someone says, “Just relax.”

Brilliant advice. Thank you. Why didn't we think of that?

Meet the thing you've been doing all day

Put one hand near your ribs, if you like, and notice how your body moves when you breathe.

Here's something odd: you can decide to take a breath, but your body also breathes while you're busy thinking about other things. It even keeps going while you're asleep.

Our breathing is automatic and something we can pay attention to. That's what makes it so interesting.

When we're excited, nervous or running about, our breathing often changes. And sometimes taking a little time to notice our breathing can help us feel more settled.

It's not magic, and it doesn't work for everyone all the time. But it's a little experiment we can try.

The one-minute reset

Find somewhere you feel comfortable. Keep your eyes open if you like.

  1. Notice three sounds around you.
  2. Feel your feet touching the floor.
  3. Let your shoulders be less tense, if they want to.
  4. For a few breaths, notice the air moving in and out normally. There's nothing to force or count.
  5. Look around the room again. Has anything changed? Maybe yes. Maybe no. Both answers count.

If paying attention to breathing feels strange or uncomfortable, skip that part. You can just notice the sounds and your feet. No breath-holding competitions. No trying to take enormous breaths.

Here's the part that matters

Feeling upset, excited, nervous or overwhelmed doesn't mean we're doing life badly. Feelings give us information; sometimes they simply need time.

We can notice what's happening inside us without having to fix everything immediately.

And if something is really worrying us, breathing isn't a replacement for telling a trusted adult or asking for help.

Next time the tabs in your mind all seem to be playing music at once, remember that there's a world outside the screen, a floor beneath your feet, and someone you can talk to.

We don't have to close every tab to find a little space.

For families and educators. This is a short, optional attention exercise for younger readers, not treatment or a challenge. Read together with children who need support. See the NCCIH overview of mind-body practices for children and teens.
