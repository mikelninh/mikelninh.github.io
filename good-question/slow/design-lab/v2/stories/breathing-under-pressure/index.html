Breathing Under Pressure.

An evidence-informed guide to stress, focus, movement and the pause between feeling and responding.

THE SLOW EDITION15 OCTOBER 20269 MIN READFULL ORIGINAL ESSAY
Type defaultCurrent edition ↗

There is a particular moment before a difficult conversation when the body gets there before the words.

The jaw tightens. The shoulders climb. The heart speeds up. Somewhere inside, the nervous system has mistaken an awkward email for a charging rhinoceros.

By the time we realise what's happening, we've often already begun rehearsing our defence.

What if we noticed earlier?

Breathing is one of the few bodily rhythms that happens automatically and can also be influenced deliberately. That's a modest fact with surprisingly useful consequences. We can't order ourselves to stop feeling anxious, but we can experiment with gentle breathing patterns that may help us feel steadier.

Not invincible. Not transformed into a productivity machine. Just a little more able to choose what happens next.

What changes when we feel threatened?

When we experience stress, our bodies prepare to respond. Breathing and heart rate may increase, muscles tense, and attention narrows. These changes are not design flaws. They're part of an ancient survival system that served our ancestors rather well.

Unfortunately, that system is not especially skilled at distinguishing a physical emergency from someone writing “Can we talk?” without further explanation.

The relationship between breathing and the nervous system runs both ways. Stress can alter breathing; changing how we breathe can also influence physiological arousal.

That doesn't make breathing a switch that instantly turns off emotion. It makes it one practical point of entry into a complicated system.

What the evidence actually says

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 31 studies, involving 1,133 participants, found that slow-paced breathing had some favourable immediate cardiovascular effects, including changes in heart rate and measures of heart-rate variability.

Another randomised study published in Cell Reports Medicine in 2023 compared short daily breathing practices with mindfulness meditation across a month. Both approaches were associated with improvements in mood; the exhale-focused breathing group had particular benefits on positive affect and respiratory rate in that study.

These results are encouraging, but they don't establish that any specific breathing pattern will work for everyone, cure anxiety disorders, or improve every aspect of athletic performance.

And one important correction to a popular internet myth: bigger, faster breaths are not automatically better breaths. Breathing far more than our bodies need can lower carbon dioxide in the blood and cause dizziness or tingling.

When in doubt, gentle beats dramatic.

For difficult conversations: a moment before we speak

Imagine the familiar urge to win an argument. We have our evidence, our excellent closing statement, and possibly a witness willing to confirm that the dishwasher really was loaded incorrectly.

Before we deliver our masterpiece, we might try this:

  1. Pause, if the moment allows.
  2. Feel our feet and let the shoulders drop.
  3. Take a comfortable, quiet inhale, followed by an easy exhale.
  4. Continue for around 30–60 seconds, perhaps allowing the exhale to be slightly longer than the inhale if that feels natural.
  5. Ask what we actually want from the conversation: understanding, repair, a boundary, a solution?

Breathing doesn't do the emotional work for us. It simply gives that work a better chance of being done intentionally.

For focus: choose one thing, then begin

We live in an age of astonishing tools and spectacular distractions. We can learn a language, watch a documentary, message a friend across the world, and lose twenty minutes to a video of a dog befriending a duck, all on the same device.

Before starting work, we can try a three-minute transition.

Sit comfortably. Let the breath find an unforced rhythm. Notice it for a short while, returning gently when attention wanders. Then write down one concrete task worth finishing.

Start there.

The breathing doesn't create focus by magic. What it creates is a ritual: a boundary between receiving everything and choosing something.

For movement: less unnecessary tension

In martial arts, dance, running or strength training, breathing changes naturally with effort. Sometimes we notice ourselves holding our breath while concentrating or tightening muscles that don't need to help.

Breath awareness can reveal those habits. Before training, spend a minute standing comfortably and noticing how your ribs and abdomen move. As you warm up, observe whether your breath flows freely.

During effort, use breathing that suits the activity and instruction from a qualified coach. Heavy exertion may require faster, deeper breathing; specialised lifting techniques can involve bracing and are not the same as slow breathing for relaxation.

There is no single breath pattern that grants Bruce Lee's timing. That particular upgrade still appears to require practice.

But becoming more aware of tension is a useful beginning.

For rest: permission to stop performing

Many of us have become so good at turning life into a project that we now evaluate the quality of our relaxation.

Was it deep enough? Did we achieve sufficient recovery? Should we buy a device to measure the effectiveness of our sitting quietly?

We might try something less ambitious.

For five minutes in the evening, sit or lie comfortably. Let your breathing settle. If it feels pleasant, experiment with an unforced rhythm such as four seconds in and five or six seconds out. If counting creates tension, forget the numbers and breathe naturally.

When the mind wanders, let it. Return when you're ready.

Rest doesn't need to become another competitive sport.

A sensible safety note. These are gentle everyday practices, not medical treatment. Never force hyperventilation or prolonged breath-holding, especially in water or while driving. Stop if you feel dizzy, distressed or unusually short of breath. People with significant heart or lung conditions, persistent symptoms, or a history of panic or trauma may prefer guidance from a healthcare professional. When breathing itself feels uncomfortable, grounding through sounds, movement or contact with the floor is a good alternative.

Seven days, one honest experiment

If we're curious whether any of this makes a difference, we can find out without turning ourselves into a science project.

  1. Day 1: Observe natural breathing for three minutes.
  2. Day 2: Relax the jaw and shoulders for three minutes.
  3. Day 3: Experiment with gentle slower breathing for five minutes.
  4. Day 4: Pause before a conversation that matters.
  5. Day 5: Notice breathing and tension during movement.
  6. Day 6: Begin meaningful work with a three-minute pause.
  7. Day 7: Repeat the practice that seemed genuinely useful.

Notice, without judging, whether your response to the day changes. A calmer feeling is welcome; having an ordinary day is equally acceptable. No streaks, badges or personal bests required.

Beyond the technique

Dan Brulé, author of Just Breathe, places awareness, relaxation and conscious breathing at the centre of his teaching. We can borrow the invitation while remaining thoughtful about what any practice can and cannot promise.

Breathing won't make us immune to grief, eliminate uncertainty, or resolve conflicts we are avoiding. Sometimes the right next step is a difficult conversation, proper rest, professional help, or simply saying no.

But every day gives us chances to notice our bodies before we act on autopilot.

The next time a message raises our pulse, an argument heats up, or a demanding day begins, we may remember something simple: there is room for one gentle breath before the response.

And in that small space, we may meet ourselves — and each other — a little more kindly.
