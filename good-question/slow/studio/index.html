Not an article factory.

01 / Why start?

I didn't want a thousand articles. I wanted help asking one question well.

At first, I thought we were building a website about veganism: the arguments, the studies, the uncomfortable counterarguments, the favourite things we cook and buy. Then I found myself wanting to write about yoga philosophy, the taste of tea, beautiful films, tiny games and whether we can live with less suffering without becoming terribly self-righteous.

A documentary prompted a deceptively hard question: if removing an invasive fish helps a threatened reef, is killing it an act of care? Ecology can investigate the outcomes of a specific intervention. Ahimsa asks us to consider the suffering and moral standing of the animal itself. Neither a tidy AI summary nor a beautiful chart should pretend to make that conflict disappear.

That's when GOOD QUESTION started to feel less like a single-topic blog and more like the seed of a publication: one place to practise curiosity, show our sources, and let readers disagree intelligently.

02 / Meet the team

Three named workflows. One accountable human.

QUILL is our proposed Editor-in-Chief role. It turns a pile of ideas into a small, legible editorial plan. PROOF is the research and verification role: find the original source, identify what it actually supports, look for counterevidence and keep the uncertainty visible. ATELIER is the design and interaction role, shaping words into experiences worth spending time with.

These are roles and instructions in our repository — not three continuously running, independently qualified employees. I can use AI to work through them. I cannot truthfully claim that a research editor has signed off just because a model checked a link.

I'm the human publisher. I choose what deserves our attention, decide where our ethical commitments belong, and remain responsible for what we publish. A scripted workflow can create a planning issue every fortnight; it cannot independently conduct interviews, certify nutrition advice or mail an approved issue to subscribers.

03 / What actually exists

There is a working magazine. There is also a very unfinished company.

The archive includes ten essays in three languages — thirty separate article pages. The previous issue includes a small ingredient-price calculator with explicit exclusions and a public source register. Our Slow Edition lets readers choose topics, reach a real end to the feed, browse a provisional product shelf, and explore a reef dilemma through an ethics game that makes no scientific predictions.

The source repository contains an editorial charter, a Codex handoff, a prioritised backlog and an automation that proposes a planning issue. The recorded Slow Edition technical suite passed 120 of 120 checks. That tests browser behaviour and links, not the scientific truth of our journalism, the quality of our Vietnamese translations or whether anyone actually enjoys reading it.

The product shelf is intentionally imperfect too. A German manufacturer may describe one Shin Ramyun variant as vegan while a Toomba formulation sold elsewhere contains milk ingredients. We cannot borrow a badge from one market for every package. The correct response is a dated, exact-product label check — not a louder recommendation.

04 / What hasn't happened

The unfinished parts are part of the story.

We have not commissioned a qualified ecologist to review our reef article. We do not yet have recorded interviews, independently witnessed taste tests, a complete accessibility audit or native-language sign-off for every piece. We have not built an email subscriber system with explicit consent, and we have no confirmed affiliate partnerships or audited revenue to boast about.

AI makes beautiful drafts dangerously easy. It can make a weak claim sound settled, a mock-up feel launched, or a product recommendation seem tested because the card looks expensive. If I start believing the surface instead of checking the substance, the whole publication loses its reason to exist.

Our answer is a public distinction between ideas, drafts, source-checked claims, human-approved work and published corrections. We will leave some questions unresolved. A thoughtful 'we don't yet know' should be a mark of care, not an embarrassment.

05 / The next two weeks

Four good pages. Then go outside.

WUNDERPOST is our proposed four-page letter every fourteen days, starting as a web edition. One substantial story, one useful thing to try, one cultural surprise and one honest studio note. No inbox emails are being sent yet. The Slow Feed is deliberately finite: choose Food, Nature, Mind, Culture or Studio and reach the end without an infinite recommendation engine demanding more attention.

For our next issue I'd rather finish one genuine label check, one credible expert conversation or one unforgettable five-minute interactive story than publish twenty frictionless AI posts. We'll ask real readers what confused them, what changed their mind, and whether they would share a piece with a friend who disagrees.

I want to measure source corrections, usefulness, reader trust and the time it takes to create something worth keeping — not merely articles per agent. If we make money through things we genuinely use and love, it will be disclosed and kept separate from editorial evidence.

It won't be perfect. It can't be. The point is not to build a machine that always sounds right. The point is to build a small place where it's safe to ask again.

Perhaps the most human thing an AI newsroom can do is make more room for human judgement.
