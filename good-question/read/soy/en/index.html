05 / FOREST & BEAN

Is rainforest cleared for your tofu?

Soy can drive deforestation. The allocation data tell a more complicated story.

GOOD QUESTION · EditorialPublished 11 October 20262 minute read
77%
THE GOOD QUESTION PAPER / 002

The question sounds like checkmate: soy is linked to forest loss. So must tofu be the villain? Follow where the bean goes before deciding.

01 / THE OBJECTION

The strongest objection

Soy cultivation can be associated with deforestation and other environmental damage. Plant-food supply chains are not magically harmless. A block of tofu is not automatically deforestation-free.

02 / THE EVIDENCE

What the evidence actually says

Our World in Data summarises global allocation data: in its 2018 breakdown, roughly 77% of soy was used as livestock feed and about 7% was used directly for human foods such as tofu, soy milk, tempeh and edamame. Oil and industrial uses account for other shares. This means demand for animal feed is central to the soy-land-use story. It cannot tell us the footprint of every individual tofu brand, but it corrects a misleading picture of scale.

A bean is not a supply chain.

Three distinct soy questions often get conflated: global land demand, the origin of a specific product, and the direct use of soy for humans versus animals. The 77% figure describes a global allocation estimated for 2018. It cannot tell you where one packet of tofu came from or guarantee that all soy farming is sustainable. Soybeans are also processed into several outputs, with oil extraction and feed uses connected, so allocation figures require care rather than careless addition. A credible consumer answer must therefore be both systemic and specific: what drives demand for cultivated land, and can the producer explain the origin of this exact product?

03 / THE LIMITS

What remains uncertain

These shares are drawn from secondary analysis of historical commodity data, not data reanalysed independently by us. Traceability varies widely from product to product.

04 / YOUR NEXT STEP

A useful next step

Prefer transparent suppliers and ask where a product's soy comes from. Most importantly, compare whole food systems rather than blaming one visible bean.
