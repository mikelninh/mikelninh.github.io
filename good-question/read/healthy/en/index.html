01 / THE BASICS

Is vegan actually healthy?

The answer is better than a simple yes or no.

GOOD QUESTION · EditorialPublished 11 October 20262 minute read
✳
THE GOOD QUESTION PAPER / 002

There is a whole nutritional universe between lentils and a packet of vegan biscuits. The word ‘vegan’ tells us what is absent — not what nutrients are present.

01 / THE OBJECTION

The strongest objection

The strongest objection: a diet that requires planning and supplementation cannot be unconditionally healthy. Neglecting nutrients such as vitamin B12, iodine or calcium can have consequences. That is a real concern, not a cheap talking point.

02 / THE EVIDENCE

What the evidence actually says

Germany’s nutrition society DGE revised its position in 2024. For healthy adults, vegan eating can be health-promoting if B12 is reliably supplemented, food choices are balanced and potentially critical nutrients are covered. The NHS likewise advises variety and fortified foods. Neither organisation claims that every vegan meal is nutritionally excellent — or that plants cannot sustain a healthy diet.

It is the pattern, not the label.

To understand the recommendation, look at what is eaten rather than whether a product says “vegan.” A diet built around pulses, whole grains and vegetables differs profoundly from one relying on sweets and snack foods. “Can be health-promoting” does not mean “automatically healthy for everyone.” The DGE specifies the conditions: sufficient energy, varied food and attention to nutrients that may be inadequate. Vitamin B12 deserves a reliable source, not hopeful reliance on unfortified plants. Iodine, calcium and other nutrients also warrant consideration. The question is not whether plants are virtuous but whether an actual diet supplies what the person needs.

03 / THE LIMITS

What remains uncertain

No single number captures every long-term health outcome. Observational studies can only partially adjust for lifestyle differences. People with additional nutritional needs require additional care.

04 / YOUR NEXT STEP

A useful next step

Start with a colourful foundation: pulses, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds. Make B12 and iodine plans explicit; seek qualified advice for individual health risks.
